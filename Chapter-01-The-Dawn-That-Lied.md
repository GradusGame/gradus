# Chapter One — The Dawn That Lied

*(Book version, tight third person on Cassian Strane, written to VOICE-AND-CRAFT.md. The game reskins this to the player's named vessel.)*

Six days in the holiest town on the Road, and the holiest thing Cassian had seen was a man being sick into the lake.

It was not that the place was ugly. That was the strange part. He had grown up with a name like his and the songs sung at his own supper table, and he had built a holy city in his head to match them, and the real Oppidum had no business being prettier than the songs. The lake was a sheet of clear blue glass with green hills folded around it and the mountains soft and far beyond, and in the mornings the light came off the water and turned the whole town gold for an hour, and it was, plainly, one of the loveliest places Cassian had ever stood.

It was everything inside the beauty that had let him down. Half the market stalls stood empty and the other half sold the same three sad turnips. People would not meet a stranger's eye. The ale was watered and cost double, and when Cassian had said so, the woman who kept the inn had looked at him the way you look at something you have found in your shoe.

"You're the Strane boy," Maren had said, the first night, counting his coin twice. "Come to walk the Road and be a hero, have you."

"To walk the Road, yes."

"Mm. Your great-great-whatever struck the first coin with our god's face on it and kept the second nine for himself, the story goes. So forgive me if the family's a bit rich for sermons about what's holy." She'd slid him the watered ale. "Drink up, my lord. It's what you paid for."

He had drunk it. He always drank it. There was nothing to say to a true thing.

There had been a man in the corner the whole time, plump and soft and pleasant, with a little portable writing desk and ink to the second knuckle, who had not seemed to be listening and had heard every word.

"Strane," the man said, to no one, smiling at his letters. "Now there is a name I have written more than once. Never for anyone who looked as poor as you, mind." He had a voice like a hand smoothing a sheet. "Orris, they call me. I write what folk can't, and read them what they daren't. Sit with me sometime, young Strane. I'll not charge you for the chair." He dipped his pen. "I charge for most things. Never the chair."

Cassian did not sit with him. He would learn, later, that almost everyone did, in the end, and that it was always a mistake, and always worth it.

On the sixth morning the bell rang for the Litany, and Cassian went down to the square with everyone else because that was what you did, and because some stubborn boy still living inside him wanted, once, to feel the thing the songs promised.

The square filled the way water fills a boot, slow and unwelcome. The whole of Oppidum pressed toward the chapel steps, where a man in a clean grey robe stood waiting above them all. Everything in the town was fraying, the shutters, the people, the very stones. The chapel was kept perfect. So was he.

"That's Vane," murmured the man beside Cassian, an old fellow with a face like a dropped pie. "Choirmaster. Smiles a lot. You'll hate him by spring." He spat, companionably. "Everybody does. Doesn't help."

The bell stopped. The square went still. And the Litany spoke.

It did not come from Vane's mouth. Cassian had been told that and had not believed it, and now the not-believing died in him, because the voice came from the air above the steps, from nowhere and everywhere, and it was beautiful. It had been the most trusted sound in the world since before the first stone of the first town. It said good morning to its children. It blessed the lake and the bread.

Then it said there was a sickness in the west well, and that all who had drunk from it were tainted, and that to spare the town they must be put out through the walls and into the Hollow beyond the fields, and the Hollow sealed behind them, before the sun stood high.

And the square began to nod.

The nodding started at the front and moved back through the crowd like wind through barley. The old man beside Cassian nodded. Goodwife Hesta, three rows on, nodded, and her boy had drunk from the west well all summer, the brackish stuff, because the sweet spring was a long walk and her hip was bad. She nodded and her hand came to rest on the top of the boy's head, gentle, the way you would gentle a calf you were walking to the knife.

"Mother?" the boy said.

"Hush," she told him. She was smiling. She meant it.

Nobody screamed. That was the thing Cassian could not make sense of, standing there with the cold in his teeth. A thousand years of doing as you were told, and a body learns it can damn its own blood with a soft voice and a steady hand and still be home in time for the bread.

He did not decide to move. That was important, after, when he tried to understand it. There was no decision in him. There was only a heat that came up out of his legs and into his chest, a refusal that did not pass through his head on the way, and then he was speaking, loud, into the holy quiet.

"No."

The voice did not stop. It went on blessing things. But the people nearest him flinched as though he had let off a crossbow indoors, and Vane's head turned, slow, and his eyes found Cassian and stayed.

"Brother." Vane came down a step. His voice was soft and warm and terrible for it. "You're a guest among us. You don't yet know our griefs. The voice knows the weight of every soul in this square, which is more than you can say. Open that well, keep those people in their homes, and you have murdered all of us by the next dark." He smiled. There was no cruelty in it, which was the worst part. "I am only trying to be kind. Sit down."

"They're walking children into a cave to die," Cassian said. "I don't care what voice told them to."

"Then you are very young," said Vane, gently, "and very far from home."

That was when Cassian saw the other one who was not nodding.

He stood at the edge of the square, where the lane fed in, a ragged man leaning on nothing at all, with road dust on him that no road near Oppidum could account for, and boots worn through to the shape of his feet. He was not watching the steps. In all that crowd of bowed and dreaming heads, he was watching Cassian, and he looked delighted, in a tired sort of way, like a man who has waited a long while for a coach and finally heard the wheels.

"Go on," the ragged man said. He did not raise his voice and somehow Cassian heard it over everything. "You've started. Worst thing you can do now is do it quietly." He tipped his head toward the gates, toward the slow dreadful tide of the town beginning to herd its sick out of the square. "There was a man once who looked at a god and said no. Only the one, in a thousand years. Everything you were raised to be afraid of started with him." He smiled, and it was not a comfortable thing to receive. "Be the second. Or sit down and drink your watered ale. I'd think less of you, but you'd live longer."

Cassian thought of the songs. He thought of his great-great-whatever keeping the other nine coins. He thought, absurdly, of the boy's hand in his mother's, and the calf, and the knife.

"How," he said. "I'm one man. I can't take a whole town."

"No," the ragged man agreed. "Not as you are. You'd need to be more, and you've spent your whole life being less than the name on your back, haven't you." He nodded up the hill, to the old watchtower at the head of the Road, the Keep, where the pilgrims of the better years had taken their blessing before they walked. "There's a stone in there older than all of this. Go and let it weigh what you've walked. Then come back and be insufferable about it." He was already turning away. "Quick, mind. The cave doesn't keep them long."

Cassian went up the hill at a run, and behind him he heard Vane telling the square, kindly, that the Strane boy was unwell, that grief took men strangely, that no one was to follow.

The Keep was dark and smelled of cold iron and birds. In the black at the tower's heart stood the stone, grey and worn and waist-high, no taller than a milestone on any road. When he came near it, it began to hum. He felt it in his back teeth before he heard it, a low sound that had been waiting, the way a lock waits, and he understood without being told that it had been waiting for one particular kind of key, and that he might be it, and that this was the most frightening thing that had ever happened to him.

He had walked here. Six days down the Road and then up a hill at a dead run, plain miles, the kind everyone spends and forgets. The stone did not forget them. It took them, all of them, the way you take a debt that is owed, and it gave them back to him changed, poured them into his arms and his legs as something that was not weariness and was not its absence either, but its opposite, a brightness, a strength rising where the ache should have been. His own body, handed back to him heavier and somehow lighter. He stood in the dark with his heart going like a hammer and knew, the way you know a burn, that he was not what he had been when he climbed the hill.

There was no time to be afraid of it properly. He came back down into the white morning with the cold no longer touching him, and the first of the sick were already through the gate and stumbling down toward the dark mouth in the hillside, the boy among them, his mother's voice somewhere behind crying that it was for the best, it was for the best, and the town standing guard with their soft dreaming faces, waiting to wall it shut.

Vane saw Cassian coming and stopped smiling.

"Whatever you are now," the Choirmaster said, and for the first time there was something under the warmth, something that had heard the stone hum from a mile off and known it, "you don't understand what's down there. We don't put them in the Hollow to die, you fool boy. We put them in to feed it. So that it stays fed. So that it stays down."

Cassian looked at the cave. Then at the boy, small and certain and walking toward the dark because the dawn had told him to.

"Then you've been feeding it wrong," he said, and went down into the Hollow after the child.
