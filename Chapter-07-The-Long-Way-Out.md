# Chapter Seven — The Long Way Out

*(Book version, tight third on Cassian. Written to VOICE-AND-CRAFT.md and the Book One throughline. Reach 1, Oppidum arc, chapter 7 of 7. The Departure: a colder look at the Vagabond, the Elder Road hum, the goodbye to Col, a warm sendoff at the Horse, the ladies-man-but-innocent trait planted, and the companion who walks out the gate with him so he does not leave alone. Hook into Reach 2.)*

They buried Brand a second time three days after the first, and this time there was a body, and Cassian thought that was the only gift he had managed to give Oppidum in the whole bloody week: a grave instead of a candle. He stood at the back of it and nobody asked him to say words, which was a mercy of its own.

He told Maren he was leaving the same night.

"I know," she said. She was wiping a clean table again, which he had learned was the thing her hands did when the rest of her was holding something down. "Of course you are. The Road came up here looking for you and it sent our Brand to do it, and it'll send worse, and you're not the kind to sit in a warm inn and let a town take the arrows for you. More's the pity. I could've used the custom." She stopped wiping. "You'll want provisioning. And you'll want to not do it alone, but I've given up telling you things, I might as well lecture the lake."

The trouble with leaving, Cassian discovered, was the women.

He had not noticed it before, being occupied with gods and monsters, but the moment word went round that the vessel was taking the Road, half of Oppidum's daughters and widows and a fair number of its wives developed urgent reasons to bring him things. Travelling bread. A mended cloak. A scarf knitted in a single feverish night by the cooper's daughter, who pressed it into his hands and went the colour of a sunset and fled. A woman he was fairly sure he had never spoken to gave him a small carved bird for luck and held his hand a moment longer than luck required. Cassian accepted all of it with the grave puzzled gratitude of a man who genuinely believed his town had simply become very generous about charity.

"They're being kind," he told Maren, baffled, arms full of bread and scarves, "because I'm leaving."

Maren looked at him for a long moment. "Aye," she said, finally. "That's it. That's the reason. You've a jaw like a temple saint and the eyes of a kicked hound and the romantic instincts of a fence post, and the whole town's broken-hearted that the prettiest disaster it's ever housed is walking off down the Road, and they're being kind. You poor honest fool. Eat your bread."

He did not understand her, which she appeared to find both maddening and, somehow, reassuring.

The Foundered Horse threw him a sendoff, because the Foundered Horse would find a reason to throw anyone anything. They put an ember in his hand and another and a third, Wick and Gorn paused their eternal cheating to bet loudly on how far down the Road he would get before something tried to eat him, Sweet Lyle sang the ballad of Gradus and the god with every single word wrong and the whole tavern corrected him at the tops of their lungs and then fought about it, and Captain the three-legged dog sat on Cassian's feet the entire night as though pinning him in place might cancel the leaving. Big Doll gave him a flask of her own ember, the real stuff, the kind that wrote to your mother, and told him it would keep the cold out for exactly one night and to use it on the worst one. For a few hours, in the loudest warmest room in the freezing world, Cassian let himself be among people who would not let him be alone, and he understood, properly for the first time, what it was he was walking away from, and what it was these people had taught him without meaning to.

Col found him at dawn by the gate, and that was the hard one.

The boy did not cry, which was worse than crying. He stood very straight and said he was coming, he had a stick and everything, he could be useful, and Cassian knelt down in the cold and did the thing his whole nature screamed against, which was to refuse to take someone he loved into danger he could carry himself. "No," he said. "Not this road. Not yet."

"You're leaving everyone," Col said, and it was not an accusation, which made it land like one.

"I'm leaving so the Road stops coming here," Cassian said. "So it stops coming for you." He pressed the worn Gradus coin back into the boy's hand, the one Col had given him in the dark inn a week ago, a lifetime ago. "Keep this for me. Mind your mother. Stay where it's loud. And when I come back, I'll want it returned, so you have to still be here, do you understand? That's the deal. You hold the coin, I come back for it." It was a promise he had no business making, and he made it anyway, because the boy needed a reason to stay alive more than Cassian needed to be honest.

The Vagabond was leaning on the gatepost as though he had grown there.

"You'll go up through the Greenwood," the ragged man said, without preamble, handing Cassian nothing, offering only the direction. "It's wild now, with no Litany to tame it, and there are people in it worth your trouble, if any are. You'll want to reach the dead city after, the Ruins, where they say a man can learn things the gods would rather he didn't." He smiled, and the smile did not reach the ancient amusement in his eyes. "I sent you into the Hollow knowing what it would cost, you understand. I'll send you worse places, knowing worse. I am not your friend, Strane. I have a war that is bigger than you, bigger than this town, bigger than every Brand the Hound will ever send home, and you are useful to it. I tell you this plainly because the ones who get lied to break wrong, and I need you to break right, if you break at all." He pushed off the gatepost. "There. Now you can't say a god's son never did you a kindness."

He was gone before Cassian could ask which god.

The Milestone was the last thing. He went up to the Wayward Keep at the head of the Road and put his hand on the old grey stone, and it hummed for him the way it had the first morning, the deep sound in the teeth, the road he had walked poured back into him as strength. But standing in the dark of the tower with his palm on the rock, Cassian noticed a thing he had been too frightened to notice the first time. The hum was older than the chapel above it. It was older than the dawn voice that had lied. It was, he felt with the certainty you feel about the age of a mountain, older than the gods the songs sang of, and the strength that came up through his arm had a taste to it of something that had been answering walkers since before there was anyone to call it holy. He did not have words for it. He took his hand away with the unsettled feeling of a man who has knocked on a door for years and only now wondered who built the house.

And there was a worse question under that one, the one he had been holding at the end of a long stick since the morning the dawn lied. Why him. Of every grieving, honest, deserving soul on the Road, why had the dead man's strength come up into a Strane, the blood of the very brother who had sold that dead man's name for coin and grown fat on the selling. If something had reached across the cold to choose a vessel, it had reached past everyone who had earned it and put itself into the descendant of the betrayer, and Cassian could not find one reason for that which let him sleep.

Edra was waiting at the gate with a pack on her back.

Cassian had not expected that, and he was not sure what to say to it, because the last time he had seen her she had been kneeling in the cold lake holding the body of the man he had killed.

"Don't," she said, before he could find anything. Her face had gone back to the hard handsome thing it had been before Brand came up the shore, the face of a woman who had decided long ago to stop hoping and had been proven horribly right. "Don't thank me, don't welcome me, and don't get the idea I'm doing this for you. I watched you put Brand down. I know it was the only thing left to do for him, I'm not a fool, and I hate you for it the right amount, which is not very much and will not go away." She shouldered the pack higher. "But he went down that Road to find out what was at the end of it, and he came back wrong, and you are the only soul I have ever met who is walking toward the same thing on purpose and might live long enough to reach it. I need to know what took him. I need to see the thing that's at the end of all this. And someone needs to walk behind you and make sure that when the dark starts whispering that it's all right to stop, you've got somebody to tell you it isn't." She looked at him flatly. "That's the job. I'm taking it. You don't get a say."

"I don't even know your name," Cassian said, which was true, and which she received with the expression of a woman who had just had every one of Maren's points about him proven at once.

"Edra," she said. "And before you do the face, no. I have buried the only man I will ever bother with. Whatever the cooper's daughter sees in you, I see a tall earnest disaster who's going to get us both killed being noble at the wrong moment, and I would sooner kiss the Hound. We're going to get along beautifully."

And the strange thing, the thing Cassian could not have explained to anyone, was that for the first time since the dawn had lied in this gorgeous fraying town, something in him eased. Not gone. Nothing here ever went. But loosened, like a held breath let partway out, because he was about to walk a long cold road toward a thing that wore dead men's faces, and for once he would not walk it by himself.

They went out the gate together as the sun came up over the lake and turned the whole town gold for its one good hour. Behind them Oppidum was warm and frightened and alive, a bad bard singing the wrong words and a whole tavern bellowing the right ones after him, a three-legged dog howling at the closing gate. Ahead the Road ran south into a country that grew colder with every league, toward a wild green wood, and past that a dead city in the desert, and somewhere far beyond all of it a thing that already knew his name.

Cassian took the first step of the long way, and the second, and Edra fell in beside him without being asked, half a pace back and to his left, where she could keep an eye on the Road and on him at once. He noticed. He said nothing about it. Neither did she. The gate shut behind them. The dog stopped howling. And then there was only the cold, and the Road, and the two of them on it, going south.
