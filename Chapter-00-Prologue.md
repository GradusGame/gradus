# Prologue — The Last Mile of Brand

*(The bang. A self-contained doom, tight third on a stranger, set some years before Cassian. Written to VOICE-AND-CRAFT.md. The man we watch fall here is the thing that comes back in Chapter Five.)*

For nine days Brand had not slept, and on the ninth day he understood at last that this was not the gift. This was the trap.

The valley around him did not have the decency to be ugly about it. It was the loveliest country he had walked in all his life, and he had walked a great deal of country since the dead chose him. A river ran bright and quick over yellow stones. Wildflowers stood to his knee, blue and white, nodding in a warm wind that smelled of crushed green. Mountains held the far edge of the world in soft blue folds. Somewhere a lark was going on about it as though nothing in the world were wrong, and a man was about to die in the middle of all that beauty, and the lark did not care, and why should it.

Brand kept walking because he could not stop. That was the joke the dead had played on him, though it had felt like a blessing when it started, back in Oppidum, back when he was the lad who would walk the whole Road and bring everyone's mother and brother home from the dark. His legs would not tire. His body would not quit. Nine days now without sleep, without rest, the gift driving him on past the point where a normal man would have lain down in the flowers and let it end, and the cruelty of it was that he wanted to lie down. He had wanted to lie down since the fourth day. The gift would not let him.

Behind him, unhurried, came the Hound.

The Iron Hound did not run. That was the worst of it. Brand had run, those first days, run like a hare, and it had bought him nothing, because the thing behind him simply walked, the way a man walks toward a chicken he has already cornered in the yard. Mordric had Brand's own gift in him, the endurance, the not-stopping, and a great deal more besides, and he had been doing this a very long time. He was never closer. He was never farther. He was just always there, a dark shape at the edge of the lovely country, patient as weather.

"You can put it down, you know," the Hound called, conversational, from somewhere too near. His voice was flat and bored. A man discussing a fence. "They never believe me. You walk and you walk and you tell yourself someone is coming. Nobody is coming, Brand. Nobody has come for any of you. I would know. I'm the one who meets them all."

Brand did not answer. He had stopped answering on the seventh day, when answering started to feel like agreeing.

He thought about Oppidum instead, because thinking about Oppidum was the only thing he had left. He thought about the lake at dawn and the smell of the baker's first loaves and a girl named Edra who had laughed at his promises and then kissed him anyway, the night he left, and told him to come back famous or not at all but to come back. He had been so sure. That was the thing he could not forgive himself, walking through the flowers with a god in his legs and a monster at his heel. He had been so sure he was the one. They had sung him out of the gates. The Second Strider, some wag had called him, half a joke, and he had taken it into his chest like a coal and carried it warm for two hundred miles, until the cold of the far Road and the silence of the dead and nine days without sleep had put it out, and now there was nothing in his chest but the walking, and the walking was not his.

"The hope is the heaviest thing you carry," the Hound said, closer now, almost gentle, which was obscene coming out of him. "Set it down and the rest gets light. I've seen it a hundred times. The body keeps going. It's only the man inside that has to stop."

And Brand, who had been so sure, felt the coal in his chest where it used to be, and felt how cold it was, and understood that the monster was not lying.

That was how it took you. Not the hands. He had braced for hands, for teeth, for some final violence he could at least have spat at. There was none. There was only a voice telling him the truth he had been running from for two hundred miles, that no one was coming, that the dead did not love him, that he had walked away from the only girl who did for a coal that had always been going to go out. The truth sat down in him like cold water filling a boot. And the part of Brand that had kept him a man, the part that hoped, simply could not hold its breath any longer, and it let go.

He stopped walking. After nine days. He stopped, in the flowers, by the bright river, and it was the most relief he had ever felt, and it was the end of him.

When he turned around, the Hound was right there, and had been for some time. Up close Mordric was almost ordinary, a big grey man with tired eyes, except for the way nothing moved in his face. He looked Brand over the way a farrier looks at a hoof.

"There," Mordric said. "That wasn't so far." Brand felt the words land somewhere deep, in the place the gift lived, and take root, and he hated them, and the hate was already going soft and far away, like a sound heard through water. "You'll do nicely. They always loved you in that lake town, didn't they. We'll keep that. We'll find a use for the face."

Brand opened his mouth to refuse. What came out was nothing. The lark was still going on overhead, and the river still ran bright over the yellow stones, and the country was still the loveliest he had ever walked, and Brand stood in the middle of all of it and felt the last warm thing in him cool to the temperature of the air, and then he was not afraid anymore, and then he was not much of anything anymore, and the two of them turned and started the long walk back toward the lake town, in no hurry at all.

It would be some years before Oppidum saw Brand again. The town would not want to believe it was him. The town would be wrong.
