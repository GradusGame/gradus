# Chapter Three — The Marked

*(Book version, tight third on Cassian. Written to VOICE-AND-CRAFT.md and the Book One throughline. Reach 1, Oppidum arc, chapter 3 of 7. "The Ground": the Footsworn, the world, and a man already trying to carry a whole town alone. Withholds the deep cosmology; the Footsworn believe the folk-legend, which is not the whole truth.)*

They buried Tam in the cold ground by the chapel wall, on the side where the sun came in the afternoons, because Maren said the boy had never once got up before noon in his life and she would not start him on early mornings now that he was dead. Nobody laughed. She had not meant it to be funny. It was just the kind of true thing that comes out of a person who is holding the rest of it down with both hands.

The town came, which surprised Cassian. He had thought, after the square, that they hated him. Some did. He could feel it on the back of his neck like a draft, the looks that said the stranger came and a boy died and those two facts would be married in Oppidum forever no matter what the order of them had truly been. But they came to the burial, the haters too, because Tam had been theirs and grief is bigger than blame, at least on the day of the digging.

The gravedigger, an enormous unbothered man named Pell, leaned on his spade while the words were said and remarked to Cassian, not unkindly, "Busy week for me. Not complaining. Slow autumn till you turned up." Then he spat, and went back to work, and it was the most honest thing anyone said all day.

Maren found Cassian that night in the empty common room, where he was sitting with an untouched cup because it seemed disrespectful to drink and worse to leave.

"Drink it," she said. "He'd be insulted, watching good ale go warm over him. He stole worse than that from my cellar weekly." She sat down across from him, heavily, a woman who had aged a year in three days. For a while she said nothing. Then: "I'm not going to tell you it wasn't your fault. You'll have noticed I don't say things that aren't true."

"It was my fault."

"It was." She let that sit, which was its own small cruelty and also a kindness, because she was not going to lie to spare him and that meant the rest of what she said could be trusted. "And it was his, for following, and mine, for raising a boy with more heart than sense, and the Choir's, for keeping a god in a hole and calling it weather. There's enough fault to go round and you grabbed the whole armful for yourself before anyone else could have a share. You'll do that, I think. Grab the whole armful." She looked at him. "It'll kill you. Probably us too."

He did not have an answer. He was getting used to not having answers in this town.

They came down to the cellar after the inn went dark, the Footsworn of Oppidum, and Cassian's first thought, uncharitable and immediate, was that they were not very much. Nine of them. An old man, two women past their middle years, a baker, a one-armed fellow, a boy not much older than Tam who would not meet anyone's eye. They had a banner they were ashamed of, rolled up in the corner, and a strongbox of the coin, and the look of people who had been waiting so long for a thing that they had stopped quite believing it would come, and now it had, and it had got a boy killed on its first morning, and they did not know whether to kneel to it or run from it.

The old man knelt to it. His name was Garrick, and he had waited fifty years.

"Forgive him," Garrick said, of Tam, and Cassian thought he meant forgive Cassian, but the old man went on, "the boy, I mean. For dying before he saw it. He'd have wanted to see it. We've all wanted to see it. A vessel. A true one." His eyes were wet and shining and a little frightening. "Do you know what you are, son? Do you know what that mark means, what you carry?"

"A man told me I carry Gradus." Cassian heard how it sounded, said flat in a cold cellar. "I don't know what that means. I'm not sure he did either."

"It means," said Garrick, and here it came, the whole folk-faith of it, the thing these nine had kept alive in cellars while the dawn voice rotted above them, "that the man who walked a god to death walks again in you. That a god cannot abide a mortal who will not kneel, and made the world cold for it, and that one day a vessel will walk the whole Road to its end and put it right. Free what's frozen. Bring back what was lost." He said it like scripture, because to him it was. "We mint his name so the High Crown can never burn it. We carry his coin like a curse on the throne. And we have waited, all our lives, in this cellar, for the one who would carry the man himself." He took Cassian's hand in both of his, which were dry and trembling. "And here you are."

It was, Cassian would think later, more dangerous than the Hollow. The Hollow had only wanted to eat him. This wanted to make him into something, and the something fit his whole life like a glove. To be worthy of the name. To be the one. He had wanted exactly this since he was a boy at his family's table listening to songs his ancestors had sold, and he felt it reach for him now, warm, and almost let it close its hand.

"I'm not your prophecy," he said, and pulled his hand back, more roughly than he meant. "I got your Tam killed inside a day. I'm a Strane. My family sold this man's name for coin while you were starving for it in cellars. Don't kneel to me. It'll embarrass us both."

That landed wrong, or right, he could not tell. Garrick looked stricken. The one-armed man laughed, a short bark, the first warmth in the room. "Well," he said. "At least he's not stupid. Last hope we had was very stupid. Went off down the Road full of himself and we never heard another word." He did not say the name. None of them did. But Cassian saw the way the room flinched around the shape of it, the way you flinch around a bad tooth, and he filed it away, the hope that went down the Road and did not come back, and he did not yet know it had a name he would meet in the dark of a dead city, wearing a face the town still kept a candle for.

The whisperer was on the cellar stair. Of course he was. Orris did not knock and was never quite anywhere you had left him.

"I'll save you the meeting," Orris said pleasantly, to the room, ink to the knuckle, smiling. "You want him to be your banner. He doesn't want to be. You'll argue about it for a month, which is a month you do not have, because there is a man walking up the Road toward this town who is not a man, and who comes when the dawn voice says a vessel's name aloud, which it did, two nights past, in case any of you were sleeping through the end of your lives." He let that settle into the cold. "I only mention it because I am fond of you all, and because the news is free tonight. It will not be free again. Nothing else is." His eyes found Cassian, mild and bottomless. "Strane. When you decide to do something foolish and noble and alone, which you will, come and see me first. I sell maps. The Road is longer and worse than these good people's songs let on, and you'll want to know where the bodies are before you add to them."

He was gone up the stair before anyone thought to ask him how he knew, which was, Cassian was learning, the entire trick of the man.

He came up out of the cellar near midnight, sick of being knelt to, and the inn above was no better, a place where the few still awake lowered their voices when he passed. He could not stand one more lowered voice. So he went out, to the one place in Oppidum he was certain would be worse, sure they would take one look at the man who got Tam killed and throw him in the lake.

The tavern did not notice him at all.

The Foundered Horse was the loudest room Cassian had ever stood in, packed to the beams, and the sign over the door showed a horse lying on its back with its four legs in the air and an expression of total contentment, as though dying had been the best decision it ever made. Inside it was smoke and stew and spilled ale and somebody, somewhere, playing a stringed instrument as though it had personally wronged them. Nobody turned to look at the marked man in the doorway. There was no room in the place for that kind of drama. There was barely room for Cassian.

A woman the approximate size of a doorway materialised in front of him, forearms like hams, an expression that had not been impressed by anything since before he was born. "You're drinking or you're leaving. There's a third option where you stand in my door letting the cold in, but I throw people through that wall, and it's a busy week to be mended."

"Ember," said Cassian, because it was what every other hand in the room was wrapped around.

"Brave." She said it the way you would say it to a man off to wrestle the lake, and filled a clay pot from somewhere under the bar, not from the barrels the ale came out of, capped it with a thick toasted froth the colour of honey, and set it down steaming. "Best ember in Oppidum."

He looked at the pot. He had got a boy killed and a god's hound was walking up the Road with his name in its mouth, and he did not have it in him to do anything cleverer than take her word for it. "Is it."

"It's the only ember in Oppidum." Something happened at the corner of her mouth that on a smaller woman might have been a smile, like watching a cliff almost do something. "But yes. Best in Oppidum. Welcome to the Horse, Strane. Sit anywhere a man isn't already." And she was gone into the crowd, leaving him to understand that she had known exactly who he was the whole time and considered it the least interesting thing about a paying customer.

At the next table two old men hunched over a board of little stones as though they had been hunched over it since the founding of the world.

"That's cheating," said the first.

"It's strategy."

"It's cheating, Gorn. You moved the stone."

"I moved it strategically."

"Forty years," the first said, to Cassian, to the room, to the gods, "I've played stones with this thief and he has never once won honestly nor lost gracefully." He squinted. "You're the vessel."

"So they tell me."

"Settle a thing." The old man, Wick, leaned in with the urgency of a man approaching the only matter that had ever truly concerned him. "When the First struck the god down, did he say something? There's them as say he had a whole speech. Lyle sings it. Lyle sings it wrong."

"LYLE SINGS IT WRONG," roared half the tavern, with the weary automatic fury of a thing shouted nightly.

In the corner a sweet-faced young bard with no discernible ear lowered his instrument with wounded dignity. "It is an interpretation."

"It is a crime," said Gorn, and threw a crust at him. It was the house religion, Cassian gathered: Lyle would sing the ballad of Gradus and the god, get a verse wrong, and the whole Foundered Horse would go to war over the true words, none of which any two of them agreed on, after which everyone drank, and an hour later it all happened again. There was no settling it. The settling would have ended the fun.

"Your lot wrote the coin-songs," Wick said, accusingly. "Stranes. So you'd know the right words."

"My family sold the songs," Cassian said. "I don't think selling them ever required getting them right."

Wick stared, then barked an enormous laugh and slapped the table so the stones jumped, which Gorn immediately exploited to move three of them. "Honest, this one! A Strane, honest! Mark the day." He nodded at the steaming pot Cassian had carried over and not yet dared to touch. "And he ordered an ember, not the dishwater. There's hope for the boy."

"Don't sip it," Gorn advised, not looking up from his cheating. "Sipping's for the froth. The froth's the kind part. What's under the froth is Doll's own, and Doll's own has put bigger men than you on the floor and written to their mothers about it after."

Cassian drank under the froth. Fire went down and bloomed in his chest and kept blooming, into his cold hands and his cold feet and the cold place behind his ribs that had been cold since the Hollow, and his eyes watered, and Wick watched him with the contentment of a man who had done this to a great many strangers and meant to do it to many more. "There he is. That's the ember face. You can't walk the Road on ale, vessel."

Then there was a warm weight against his shin, and a three-legged dog was looking up at him with the bottomless patient love of a creature that has already decided your stew is its stew, and while Cassian looked at the dog his bowl was, silently and somehow, empty.

"That's Captain," said Gorn, not looking up. "War hero. He'll tell you so himself if you stand him an ale. Don't. He can't hold it."

A weaselly man two seats down was very obviously not-drinking and very obviously listening, and Wick caught Cassian noticing and leaned in, delighted, in a stage whisper meant to carry: "That's Pinch. Choir's man. Reports everything to Vane." He raised his voice. "Pinch! Tell the Choirmaster the vessel's raising an army of three-legged dogs to march on the chapel. Captain's a general now. We voted." Pinch went red and scribbled something anyway, and the whole table howled.

And somewhere in the middle of all of it, the noise and the cheating and the bad song and the dog wearing his dinner and the spy taking notes on a canine general, the thing in Cassian's chest that he had carried alone since the Hollow shifted, just slightly. Not lifted. It did not lift. But for the length of one wrong verse, in the loudest warmest room in the freezing world, he was not carrying it by himself, because nobody in the Foundered Horse would have allowed it. They were too loud. They were too alive.

Big Doll threw him out personally, gently, with one ham-hand, at a time of night she described as "when the lies get boring." He walked back to the inn under a sky thick with stars, almost sorry, for the first time since he came to this town, to be alone again.

He was not alone for long. The boy was waiting for him by the dead hearth. Col. Six years old, motherless no, his mother lived, Cassian reminded himself, Hesta lived, he had saved that much, but the boy had taken to him the way a duckling takes to the first thing it sees, and there was no cruelty in the world equal to the trust on that small face. Col held up a thing in both hands. A coin. A Gradus, worn smooth.

"Mam says you're him," the boy said. "She says you'll fix the cold."

Cassian looked at the coin, the walking figure stamped on it, his own ancestor's grift and the town's whole desperate faith pressed into a disc of cheap metal, and he thought about telling the boy the truth, that he did not know how to fix anything, that he had got a better boy than himself killed yesterday, that the people who knew him best had knelt and the man who knew the world best had basically told him he was going to die badly and alone. He thought about all of it.

"I'll try," he said. It was the first promise he had made in this town. He meant it, which was the worst thing about it.

It was Hesta who found the first of the rescued in the grey morning, the old well-woman they had pulled living out of the Hollow two days before, sitting up in her bed with her eyes open and her hands folded neat in her lap, looking at the door as though she had been waiting for someone to come through it. She was cold. She had been cold for hours. There was not a mark on her, and the look on her face was not fear. It was that soft, far, dreaming look the whole square had worn the morning the Litany lied, the look of someone who had been told to lie down, and had agreed.

The thing in the Hollow was dead. Cassian had killed it himself. He had watched it come apart in the black water.

Something else had told her to lie down.
