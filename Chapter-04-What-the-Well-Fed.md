# Chapter Four — What the Well Fed

*(Book version, tight third on Cassian. Written to VOICE-AND-CRAFT.md and the Book One throughline. Reach 1, Oppidum arc, chapter 4 of 7. The Complication and the first true twist: Cassian's victory was a mistake, force is the wrong tool, and the thing that took the well-woman cannot be stabbed. Carries a warm beat in the middle per the standing rule.)*

By the third morning, six were dead, and not one of them had a mark.

They died the same way every time, which was the part that emptied the town of what little nerve it had left. They were found sitting up, or lying down neat, hands folded, eyes open, that soft far dreaming look on the face, the look of someone who had been told to stop and had been glad to. No struggle. No wound. The old well-woman first, then a fisherman who had been pulled living from the Hollow, then a widow who had not been near the Hollow at all, then three more in a single grey dawn, scattered across Oppidum like the dark had thrown a handful of seed and was waiting to see what came up.

Cassian did what Cassian did. He went looking for the thing to kill.

He took his grandfather's name and the borrowed strength and he went back down into the Hollow with a torch and a hard certainty, because he had killed the last horror that lived in this town and he would kill this one too, and he was so sure of it that he was halfway to the black water before the sureness began to curdle. The Tithe-Eater's carcass was still there, gone to grey slick rot, exactly as dead as he had left it. He had not imagined that. He had killed it. And beyond it, where the gallery narrowed and the cold got into the teeth and the torch guttered for no draft Cassian could feel, there was a place in the rock that was not a place. A seam. A wrongness the eye slid off of. Cold came out of it, and with the cold came a sound that was almost the dawn voice and almost not, a low patient murmur with no words in it, and the murmur did not want to eat him.

It wanted him to sit down. It wanted him to put the torch aside and fold his hands and rest, he had walked so far, he had carried so much, he could set it all down here in the quiet and never have to be enough for anyone again. It was the most reasonable voice he had ever heard. It was, he realised with the hair going up on his arms, the kindest thing anyone had said to him since he came to this town, and there was nothing in the gallery to swing a sword at. There never had been. He had brought a blade to fight a feeling.

He got out. He is not too proud, the older Cassian, to say that he got out fast, and that he did not stop shaking until he was up in the daylight, and that the daylight did not help as much as he wanted it to.

Choirmaster Vane was waiting at the mouth of the Hollow, because of course he was, and for once he was not smiling, and that was somehow worse than all the smiling.

"You felt it," Vane said. Quiet. Almost gentle. "Now you know. We did not put the sick in the dark because we are cruel, Strane, whatever your friends in the cellar tell you. We put them in to feed the thing that sat in that seam, so that it stayed fed, and full, and quiet, and asleep in the mouth of the deeper dark, plugging it like a cork in a bottle of bad wine." His voice did not rise. It did not need to. "For a hundred years the Choir fed the Eater so the Eater would keep its place. A few each season. A cruelty, yes. A small, known, countable cruelty, that kept the larger one corked." He looked at Cassian almost with pity. "And then a stranger came and was a hero, and pulled the cork, and now the whole bottle is pouring out into my town, and the dead are dying with smiles on. So. Was it worth a boy and a well-woman to be brave in front of a crowd? I am asking. I genuinely wish to know."

The horror of it was that Cassian could not answer, and the worse horror was that Vane was not entirely wrong, and the worst horror of all was the look on the man's face, which was not triumph and was not malice. It was grief. Vane believed every word. Vane had been corking the bottle for thirty years and calling it faith and had watched a stranger undo it in a morning, and he was not a villain in his own telling. He was the only one who had understood the price, and paid it, and now nobody would pay it, and the town would drink itself to death dreaming.

That was the day Cassian learned that the man he most wanted to be nothing like was the only one in Oppidum who had not been lying to himself.

He could not put the cork back. There was no cork. He had killed the cork. And a sword was no use against a murmur in the rock that asked you, kindly, to rest. He stood in the daylight with the borrowed strength humming uselessly in his arms, the strength that had run a god to ground and broken the Tithe-Eater and carried a dead boy up out of the dark, and for the first time in his life he had no idea what to do with it. That was new. He did not like it at all.

He found out the dreaming had come for Maren when the inn went quiet.

She was at the long table in the dark common room with a cup gone cold in front of her, and she was not weeping, and she was not raging, and that was how he knew. The barbs were gone. The sharp tired life of her was gone. She had that soft far look, the dreaming look, and when he said her name she turned her head slowly, like a woman surfacing from deep water she had been happy in.

"It's all right," Maren said, in a voice with no wood left in it. "It told me. It's all right to be tired, Cassian. I raised a boy and I buried him in nine days and I am so tired, and it says I can stop, and it's right, it's the first true thing anyone's said to me since they brought him up out of that hole. I think I'll just sit a while." She smiled. It was the most frightening thing he had ever seen, worse than the Tithe-Eater, worse than the seam in the rock. "I think I'll just sit."

And Cassian, who fixed things alone, who carried things alone, who had carried her boy out of the dark by himself and would not let anyone share the weight, did the one thing that was the opposite of all of that, because it was the only thing the murmur had no answer for. He did not try to fix her. He could not fix her; there was nothing to swing at. He sat down. He took her cold hands in his, and he held on, and he started talking, about nothing, about Tam stealing from her cellar, about the ember and Wick's cheating and the dog that ate his dinner, and when she tried to drift he squeezed her hands hard enough to hurt and said her name and would not let her go quiet, would not let her be alone in it, not for one breath.

He sent the boy Col running, and Col came back with the Foundered Horse, with Big Doll and Wick and Gorn and half the loud warm impossible heart of that tavern, and they crowded into Maren's dark inn and they made noise at her, sang at her badly, argued about the songs over her, put an ember in her cold hands and folded her fingers round it, and refused, all of them, loudly, to let her sit quiet and be told it was all right. It is hard to dream that the kind thing is to stop when there is a three-legged dog standing on your feet and a bad bard butchering a ballad two inches from your ear. The dreaming wants you alone. It cannot work a crowd.

She came back. Near dawn, the wood came back into her voice, and she swore at all of them for the racket, and wept at last, finally, where they could see, and was held, and lived.

But six were dead who had died alone, and the seam in the rock was open and pouring, and there was no cork in the world for it now. Cassian stood in the doorway of the inn in the grey light, listening to a town learning that it could only keep its people alive by never letting one of them be alone in the dark again, and he understood that this was not a thing he could carry out of Oppidum on his back, and that he was going to have to leave anyway, because the murmur in the rock was only the first cold breath of the thing at the end of the Road, and the thing at the end of the Road was awake now, and it knew his name.

Somewhere out past the lake, much nearer than it had been, a big grey man stopped walking for the night, sat down on a milestone that was not the holy one, and waited for the morning with the patience of a thing that has never once needed to hurry.
