# Interludes — The Founding Days

*Dramatized scenes from before the fall, centuries gone now, told close and warm. A different register from the chronicle in THE-BOOK-OF-GRADUS.md and written to VOICE-AND-CRAFT.md. In the game they unlock as memory-fragments; in the novel they are interlude chapters. The job is simple and it is the most important job in the project: make the reader love these two while they are still alive and ordinary and badly dressed, so that everything cold afterward has something warm to grieve.*

---

## What She Saw

Every midsummer the fair brought the same parade to the healer's door, and every midsummer Maelis wanted to drown herself in the rain barrel by noon.

They came to court her. Men from the next valley, from the market town, once a fellow from clear over the river who turned out to be married. They brought ribbon and honey and poems they had clearly bought, and they stood in Aregund's yard with their chins up and their good words loaded, and they performed. That was the thing. Every one of them performed.

A healer learns young to read what a body hides under what it shows. Maelis had carried that out of the sickroom and into the rest of her life without meaning to, and it made the courting a small daily misery, because she could see the wanting under every pretty word. Not love. Wanting. The way they totted up her face and her hair like a good field going cheap.

"You could just marry one," said Wyn, her sister, watching the latest one go with a look that was not entirely kind. Wyn was plainer, and younger, and nobody bought Wyn poems. "Pick the rich stupid one. Leave the rest for the rest of us."

"You have him," said Maelis.

"He smells of eel."

"Then you'll always know where he is."

She noticed Gradus because he was the only man on the green not looking at her. He was down on one knee in the dirt mending a neighbour's cracked cartwheel, taking his time, and when the work was done he did not wait to be thanked. He stood, looked off down the road out the far end of the village the way a thirsty man looks at a well, and walked off down it. For no reason she could see. Just to walk.

She found a reason to cross the green.

He answered her plainly, which undid her more than any poem could have managed. She asked him a thing and he gave her the true answer instead of the flattering one. She made a joke and he thought about it, actually thought, before he laughed, and then he laughed for real. The whole time she watched him with the sight she could not switch off, waiting for the false note, the wanting under the words.

There wasn't one. She looked and she looked and there was no bottom to find, because the man was exactly as much as he showed and not a coin more. For a woman who saw through everyone, it was the strangest thing in the world, standing in front of someone and hitting wood all the way down.

"You're staring," he said.

"I know," she said, and kept doing it.

## The Long Way to the Well

The summer the near well went brackish, the whole of Wendholt drank it anyway and complained, because the near well was near and a person has forty things to do before noon.

Gradus would not drink it, and would not let Maelis drink it either. Every dawn before the fields he walked out past the east meadow to the old spring under the white stones, which was a long way, and filled a clay jar with water cold enough to ache your teeth, and carried it back and set it by her door. Then he went and did his own day's work.

"It's a foolishness," she told him on the third morning, leaning in the doorway. "The brackish won't kill anyone. Half the Reach drinks worse and breeds like rabbits. You'll wear your feet to the ankle over a jug of water."

"It's not far," he said.

It was a very long way. They both knew exactly how long. That was the joke neither of them was laughing at.

"You have a horse," she said. "A perfectly good horse. It stands in the field all day thinking up insults about you."

"The horse doesn't know where the sweet water is."

Which was not an answer, and was. He picked up his tools and looked at her the way he looked at the road, that plain open want, and went off to the fields. She stood in the door with the cold jar at her feet. He did not say he loved her. He never once said it. He walked the long way every dawn so she would never have to taste anything bitter, and he would have called that not far until the day he died, and he would have meant it.

She drank the sweet water. She always drank the sweet water.

## Crossing the Ice

He had gone three days' walk to the market town to trade the autumn wool, promising he would be home for the feast, and then the winter came early and hard and shut the world.

A true winter, that one. An honest cold with no god in it, though nobody knew yet that there was any other kind. The river below Wendholt froze bank to bank in a single black night, and the elders said no one would cross before the thaw, and they said it the way the elders say things, as if saying it settled it.

Maelis sat at the window with the shutter cracked and watched the white road.

The women told her, kindly, that he would shelter in the market town and come when the ice broke, that only a fool tried a frozen ford, that she should bank the fire and stop her staring. She thanked them, kindly, and went on staring, because they did not know what she knew. He had said he would be home for the feast. In all the years she had known him he had never once not been where he said he would be. It was not stubbornness and it was not pride. When Gradus said a thing, the thing came true, slowly, by him simply arriving, the way dawn comes true.

At dusk she saw the shape out on the river.

It came off the far bank onto the ice and it did not hurry and it did not slip and it did not stop. It walked the frozen ford the way he walked everything, one sure step and the next and the next, the pack of traded goods on its back as if the ice were only more road, which to him it was. She was out the door and down the bank without her cloak before she had decided to move, and met him as he came up off the river, his beard white with frost, his hands like stone, grinning at her like a boy who has got away with something.

"It's not far," he said, before she could start, because he knew exactly what she was winding up to say.

She hit him on the frozen arm. It hurt her hand more than it hurt him. Then she held on and would not let go, and they stood a moment on the bank in the dark with the river hard and silent under them, and went up to the fire and the feast, the two of them, in out of the cold.

The ice held that night. He came home over it the way he had promised. That is the part to keep, for later, when there is a different winter and the ice does not hold and she is the one out on it. Keep this one. They earned it.

## What She Could Not Mend

The Aldermere girl had been in labour two days when they finally came for Maelis, which was a day and a half too late, and Maelis knew it the moment she ducked through the low door and smelled the room.

She worked until dawn anyway. She was good, the best in three valleys, and she had the sight on top of the skill, which meant she could feel the wrongness in a body the way you feel a wrong note in a tune, and feeling it did not always mean she could mend it. She got the child out alive. She could not stop the mother bleeding. She had her hands inside another woman's life up to the wrist and all her cleverness and all her sight, and the woman went grey and then white and then still under her hands while the new daughter cried in the corner, and there was a moment, a long one, where Maelis simply could not understand it. She had seen the problem. She had seen it clearly. Seeing a thing and saving it were supposed to be the same act. They were not. They had never been. She had just always been lucky enough not to learn the difference in public.

She came home at full light with blood dried to her forearms and did not answer when Gradus spoke to her. She stood in the middle of the room. Then she started cleaning a pot that was already clean, scrubbing at it, and when he reached for her she said, "Don't," in a voice he had never heard her use, and he stepped back, and she scrubbed the clean pot, and her shoulders were shaking.

"I saw it," she said, to the pot. "I saw exactly what was killing her. I could have drawn you a picture of it. What good is it. What is the use of seeing a thing if you cannot." She did not finish. She set the pot down very carefully, as though it were the thing that might break.

Another man would have told her it was not her fault. Gradus knew, in his slow sure way, that it was the worst thing he could say, because it was true and being true would not help and would only prove he had not understood. Another man would have held her. He could see she did not want to be held, that being held would make her cry, and that she had decided, in some hard private place, that she was not going to cry over this where it could be seen.

So he did not say it was not her fault, and he did not hold her. He put on his coat.

"Walk with me," he said.

"It's barely light. I'm covered in—" She stopped. "I can't."

"I know," he said. "Walk with me anyway. Not far."

It was, of course, very far. He took her out along the river in the cold new morning and he did not talk, because talking was not the thing on offer, and he set a pace she could match without thinking about it, and they walked. The walking did what walking did for him and what he had never once found the words to explain, which was that it took the thing that was too big to sit still with and stretched it out thin enough along a road that a person could carry it one step at a time. She cried somewhere in the second mile, where the bank hid them, and he did not look at her, which was the kindest thing he had ever done, and they kept walking, and by the time they turned back the sun was properly up and she was empty and exhausted and could speak again.

"You always think the answer is to walk," she said. Her voice was raw but it was hers again.

"It usually is," said Gradus, who believed this completely, and would go on believing it long past the point where it was true, all the way to the end of him.

She took his arm for the last mile. Neither of them said anything about the Aldermere girl, or the daughter, or the thing she had seen and could not stop. There would be time for all of that, they both thought. There is always more time. It is the one thing everyone is sure of, and the one thing nobody has.
