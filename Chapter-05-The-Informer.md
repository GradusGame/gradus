# Chapter Five — The Informer

*(Book version, tight third on Cassian. Written to VOICE-AND-CRAFT.md and the Book One throughline. Reach 1, Oppidum arc, chapter 5 of 7. The Cost and the second twist: there is a leak in the cell, it is the most sympathetic man in it, and there is no clean answer. The Foundered Horse has become the town's refuge, which keeps the warmth present and pays off Chapter Four.)*

The dreaming had changed how Oppidum slept, which was to say it had stopped.

Nobody would be alone in the dark now, not after the six. The whole town had worked out the rule without anyone writing it down: the murmur could not take you in a crowd, could not whisper you to rest over the noise of other people refusing to let you, and so every night the Foundered Horse filled past its beams and stayed full till dawn, the frightened and the grieving packed in under the lamplight with the cheating and the bad songs, because the safest place in a haunted town turned out to be the loudest one. Big Doll had stopped throwing anyone out. She just kept pouring, and let them sleep where they sat, and Captain the three-legged dog went table to table all night making sure no one got too quiet. It was, Cassian thought, the strangest siege he could imagine. A town holding off the dark with watered ember and an argument about song lyrics.

It was also where the Footsworn met now, in the back, because there was nowhere private left and nowhere private was safe.

And the Choir was a step ahead of them at every turn.

A cache of coin moved in secret, and the Choir's men were waiting for it. A family the cell tried to smuggle out before Vane could name them to the dreaming, gone, taken in the night, the cell's plan known before the cell had finished making it. Worst of all, Vane had begun, softly, from the chapel steps each grey morning, to give the frightened town a way out of its fear. The stranger uncorked this, he said. The Strane boy and his pride. Give him to the thing that is coming up the Road for him, and perhaps the dark goes quiet again. It was a lie and it was also, Cassian was learning to admit, partly not, and a town drinking itself sleepless against a whispering dark is not a town that weighs lies very carefully.

Someone was telling the Choir everything. Cassian went to the one man in Oppidum who was always telling everyone everything.

Orris had a table to himself at the Horse, which no one else in the crush of the place managed, ink to the knuckle, untroubled by the end of the world going on around him. "Strane," he said warmly, not looking up from a letter he was writing for a weeping woman who could not. "Sit. Mind the chair leg, it bites. You want to know who whispers to the Choir." It was not a question. "Everyone whispers to me, which means I know who whispers to whom. It is the whole of my trade and the reason I will outlive all of you."

"Tell me."

"I sell, I don't tell. But." Orris finished the woman's letter with a flourish, blew on the ink, handed it over, refused her coin with a small gracious wave, and only then looked at Cassian with eyes like two flat coins. "But I find, in this one case, I will give it away, which I never do, and you should be frightened by that rather than grateful. I am not being kind. I am curious what you will do, and curiosity is the only thing I have never worked out how to charge for." He leaned in. "It is the baker. Edrin."

Cassian knew Edrin. Everyone knew Edrin. A soft round widower who baked the bread the whole town ran on, who had stood in the Footsworn cellar with his hands floury and his eyes kind, who had wept honestly at Tam's grave. One of the nine. One of Maren's own.

"Why," Cassian said.

"Now that," said Orris, "is the only question that ever matters, and almost no one thinks to ask it. They hold his daughter." He said it without weight, which was its own kind of weight. "Wenna. Seven years old. Vane had her name written into the dreaming's list, the next sweep, unless her father kept the Choir informed of his friends. So he kept them informed. He betrayed nine people he loves to keep one he loves more. I have watched men do far worse for far less." He returned to his letters. "What you do about it is your business. But you came to this town and pulled a cork, Strane, and a great many small decent people are drowning in what came out, and Edrin the baker is only the one whose drowning you can see. Mind the chair leg on your way up."

They took Edrin that night, in the back of the Horse, quietly, while the front of the tavern roared on against the dark, and it was the worst hour Cassian had spent in Oppidum, and he had spent some bad ones.

The baker did not deny it. That was the unbearable part. He stood in the lamplight with his floury decent hands shaking and he did not deny one word of it, and when Garrick, fifty years a believer, asked him how, just how, in a voice that had broken in the middle, Edrin only said, "She's seven. She's all I've got left of her mother. What would you have given them? Tell me honestly, Garrick, you old saint, and then tell me you'd not have done the same, and I'll call you a liar to your face, because I have buried a wife and I will not bury her too, not for nine of you, not for ninety."

The cell came apart in front of Cassian. The one-armed man, Hald, wanted him dead, said so plainly, said a leak that took whole families had to be stopped with the only thing that stopped a leak for certain, and three of them stood with him, because three of them had lost people to the Choir's foreknowing and grief wants somewhere to put itself. Garrick wept and said they were not murderers, they were not the Choir, the whole point of them was that they were not the Choir. And Maren, who two nights past had nearly let the dark whisper her down into rest, and had been hauled back into the loud living world by people who refused to let her go alone, Maren looked at Edrin and at his shaking hands and said, very quietly, "I'd have done it. For mine. I'd have done it and worse." And then she could not say anything else.

They all turned to Cassian. Of course they did. He was the vessel. He carried the man who walked a god to death. He would know what was just.

The old hard instinct rose the way it always rose, clean and certain: take it on himself, decide it alone, bear the terrible necessary thing so that no one else had to. It was the same instinct that had walked him into the Hollow by himself and got a boy killed. Knowing that did not make it any quieter. It never would.

He went with the smaller, far less certain thing under it instead, mostly because he could not stomach the alternative.

"No," Cassian said. Not a verdict. Just no. "I'm not putting a man in the ground for loving his daughter more than he loves us. That's the Litany's kind of justice, a list with a name on it and a soft voice calling it necessary, and if we keep that list we're a smaller Choir with a nicer banner." He did not feel wise saying it. He felt like a man guessing in the dark and hoping the floor was where he had left it. He turned to Edrin before anyone could argue him out of it. "I'll get Wenna out. Tonight. Myself. Somewhere the Choir can't reach her, and then there's nothing left to make you betray anyone."

"Yourself," Maren said, flat. "Past the whole Choir. Alone." She had said the rest of it already, four nights ago, over a dead boy, and watched it fail to take. She did not waste it again. "Of course you will."

He did not change his answer. The certainty had only moved. He had pointed it somewhere kinder and let himself believe that was the same as setting it down.

"They'll know it was us," Hald said. "The moment that child's gone, Vane knows the leak's cut. He gives you to the Hound by morning."

"He's giving me to the Hound anyway," Cassian said. "I'd rather it bought a child."

Sparing Edrin cost them three of the nine, who would not share a cell with a man they thought should hang, and walked out into the dangerous dark to prove it. Grief did that, it turned out. Cassian had not known.

They got Wenna out before dawn. That part went right, which Cassian had learned not to trust. Edrin held his daughter in the cold lane outside the Horse and wept into her hair and could not look at any of them, and the girl, seven and confused and half asleep, asked her father why he was crying, and he said, "Because everything's all right now, Wen. Everything's all right." Which was a lie, and the first kind one anyone had told in Oppidum in a long time.

And it was Col, of all of them, small Col who trailed Cassian like a duckling and missed nothing, who came running from the lake road with his eyes huge, to say that there was someone coming up the shore in the grey light. A man, walking. Walking strangely, he said. Not hurrying. And the night-watch on the wall had taken one look and gone white and would not say why, except that an old woman up there had started to cry, and was saying a name, over and over, a name the town had not said aloud in years, a name they kept a candle lit for in the chapel.

Cassian had never heard the name before. But he watched the way it moved through the waking town as Col said it, watched it turn the frightened faces grey, and he understood that whatever was walking up the shore toward Oppidum in the dawn, the town knew it, and had loved it, and had buried it, and it was coming home.
