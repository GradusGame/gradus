# Chapter Two — The Hollow

*(Book version, tight third on Cassian Strane, written to VOICE-AND-CRAFT.md and the Book One throughline. Pays off the Chapter One cliffhanger; first blood for Cassian's flaw. Reach 1, Oppidum arc, chapter 2 of 7.)*

The cold in the Hollow was a different cold than the morning's. It had teeth and a smell, wet rot and old iron and something underneath both that Cassian had no word for and would later wish he had never learned. The dark came up to meet him. He went down into it anyway, one hand on the dripping stone, following the small frightened sounds of the herded.

A scuff behind him. Not a sick man's shuffle. A young man's quick foot.

"My lord. Wait." It was the lad from the inn yard, the one Maren kept and fed and cuffed about the ears, Tam, sixteen and stringy and holding a wood-axe like he had decided this was the morning he became something. "I'm coming. You'll want a second pair of hands down there."

"Go back up."

"No, my lord." His chin was doing the same thing Cassian's own had done in the square an hour ago. "Nobody's done a brave thing in this town since before I was born. I'm not missing it because you told me to."

Cassian should have walked him back to the mouth by the collar. He knew it the way you know a thing in the body before the head catches up. But the child Col was somewhere down in the wet dark and there was no time, and so Cassian did the thing that he would do all his life, the thing that felt like courage and was at least half pride: he decided he would simply have to keep the boy safe himself, on top of everyone else. He would carry that too.

"Stay behind me," he said. "Touch nothing. If I tell you to run, you run, and you do not look back to see if I am running."

"Yes, my lord," said Tam, who was already not going to do that.

They found the sick first. A dozen of them, pressed into a cold gallery where the floor went slick and the walls wept, the marked of Oppidum, the well-drinkers, herded down and left for the dark the way you leave milk for a cat you do not want to admit you are keeping. Col was among them, small and white-faced, and when he saw Cassian his mouth opened to say something and no sound came out, because by then they could all hear it.

It came up out of the black water at the bottom of the gallery the way oil comes up through a wound. The Tithe-Eater. Cassian's mind refused it for a moment, refused the size and the wrongness and the pale blind hugeness of the thing, a creature that had crept through some crack between the worlds an age ago and been fed, patiently, lovingly, every month, by a town that called its cowardice mercy. It opened a mouth that had been promised one more meal. It was not in a hurry. Nothing that has always been fed is ever in a hurry.

What happened then, Cassian could never afterward put in an order that made sense. He remembers the strength. That was the part nobody had warned him of, because nobody had known to. The thing struck at him with force enough to fold a door and he took it across the shoulder and did not break, did not even fall, and somewhere far off and cold a debt was being paid into him, two days and six miles and a dead run up a hill, all of it pouring back into his arms as something that was not his and answered to him anyway. He hit the thing. It was like hitting a wall that screamed. He hit it again. He got between it and the sick and he held there, and for one bright impossible moment he thought, I can do this, I can hold all of it, I am enough.

He was not watching the boy.

He had told Tam to stay behind him and Tam had, mostly, until a sick woman slipped on the wet stone and went down at the edge of the dark, and Tam, sixteen and stringy and full of the bravest worst instinct there is, darted past Cassian to drag her clear. It took less than a breath. The Tithe-Eater did not need a breath. Cassian turned at the wrong sound and the thing was already drawing back from where the boy had been, and the boy was not making any sound now, and the wood-axe lay by itself on the wet stone.

He killed the thing. He wants that on the record, the older Cassian, the one telling it: he killed it. He went into it with the borrowed strength and his grandfather's name in his blood and he did not stop until the pale hugeness came apart in the black water and the screaming ended and the cold lost its teeth. He got the sick out. He got Col out, alive, into his mother's arms in the white morning, and the boy lived, and that was real and it mattered.

He carried Tam out himself. He would not let anyone else take the body, would not even let them share the weight of it, and if the town had no word yet for the thing they were watching him do, neither did he.

Maren was at the mouth of the Hollow. She had come running when she heard, the way you run when the boy you raised has gone somewhere brave. She looked at what Cassian was carrying and her face did a thing he hoped never to see a face do again, and she did not scream and she did not weep, not there, in front of the town. She just said, quietly, in a voice scraped down to the wood, "I'd have come. You only had to wait. The whole town would have come, if you'd waited and asked. He didn't have to go down there alone with you."

Cassian had no answer. She had not left him room for one, and she had not meant to.

The square had filled again. Some wept and took back their living. Some looked at the dead boy and then at Cassian with a fear that had not been there at dawn, because he was the one who had defied the Litany and the Litany had been right about the Hollow being deadly, and grief is a poor judge of whose fault anything is. And Vane, white and shaken and recovering his feet and his certainty together, found his soft voice and lifted it over them all.

"You see," the Choirmaster said, and he did not even sound cruel, he sounded sad, which was worse. "I told you it kept us safe. I told you, and a boy is dead for it. This is what comes of a stranger's pride. This is what comes of saying no."

It might have turned them, then. It very nearly did.

But the ragged man was there, at the edge of it as he was always at the edge of it, and he spoke without raising his voice and the square heard him anyway. "The boy is dead because a god has been eating this town for a hundred years and you called it the weather," said the Vagabond, to Vane, pleasantly. Then, to all of them, nodding at Cassian, at the blood on him and the dead lad in his arms and the live child at his mother's breast: "And that one walked down into the dark after your children, which not one of you has done in living memory. You can be afraid of him if you like. I would be. But know what you are afraid of." He looked at Cassian for a long moment, and something in the look was almost kind and almost a warning. "You carry Gradus, boy. The man who walked a god to death. They'll want you to be him. Try not to oblige them too quickly. It killed him too."

And he was gone, between one blink and the next, the way the dust on him had always said he would be.

That night the bell did not ring for sleep. In the empty square the Litany spoke into the dark to no gathered crowd, once, and what it spoke was a name. Cassian's name. His own, and the cheapened famous one behind it, said in that beautiful borrowed voice that had lied at dawn, the way you say a thing you intend to come back to.

Far down the Road, in a colder place, a big grey man with tired eyes and nothing moving in his face lifted his head, the way a hound lifts its head, and turned it toward the lake town, and began, in no hurry at all, to walk.
