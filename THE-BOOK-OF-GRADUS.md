# The Book of Gradus

*The lore retold as a book, in the voice of an in-world chronicler (a Wakeful scribe). A living document, updated as the lore grows, and written to VOICE-AND-CRAFT.md: grounded, dark, dry, asymmetric, partial. The chronicler is not a neutral narrator. Treat them as one more Account that happens to be long.*

---

I have heard the story of Gradus sung six ways, and five of them were paid for. The priests tell it one way and the Footsworn another, and the singers tell it however the room is buying, and somewhere under all of it is a thing that actually happened to actual people a very long time ago. I have spent my life scraping at the paint to find the wood. This is what I have got. Believe the parts that cost me something to write.

## One — The thousand quiet years

Before the gods, the Road. Nobody likes that sentence, least of all the gods, but the stones hold to it. There is a grey rock in the Keep at Oppidum, worn down to the shape of a loaf, older than the chapel and older than the Litany and, if you credit the deep histories, older than the Crowns themselves. Walk far enough and it will hum at you. Long before any voice spoke from any dawn, the walking was already holy, and it answered. We call it the Elder Road now, when we call it anything. The gods did not make it. They found it, and built their house on top of it, the way you build a mill on a river you did not dig.

The gods folk knew were five. Four they called the Crowns, one to each trade. Valdric the Iron, who made the first sword and looked after fighting men. Myrren the Veiled, who kept the deep words and the workers of them. Aelric Far-Sight, who strung the first bow. Seraphine the Kind, who minded the gentle trades and sat with the dying, and was the only one of the five that the dying ever loved. Over all four sat the fifth, Caelvorn, the High Crown, husband to Seraphine, and his was the voice that spoke the Litany at every dawn in every town.

For longer than anyone wrote down, that voice ran the world. The gods kept off the soil. They did not need to walk it. Each morning the Litany told the people when to plant and when to wed and when to keep indoors, and the people did as they were told, and they did not starve. A thousand years of mornings and not one soul woke up wondering what else it might do with the day. They were fed. They were safe. They were never once free, and they did not miss it, because you cannot miss a thing you have never had.

That is the part the songs get wrong. They make it sound like a golden age. It was a comfortable cage with very good food, and the door was never tried because no one knew it was a door.

## Two — The god who came down, and the woman who would not kneel

No god had ever walked the world, until Caedmon did.

He was Caelvorn's son and Seraphine's, a boy-god who had never in his long life met death, or consequence, or the word no. Golden. Bored in the particular way of someone who has been given everything and earned none of it. He came down through a door that should not have opened, to see what the small quick people did with their handful of years, and the first thing that undid him was not the woman. It was the bread, and the mud, and a miller shouting about the weight of a sack. He had never seen anything imperfect before. He stood in Wendholt gawping at the ordinary like a child at a fair.

Then he saw her, and the gawping stopped.

Maelis. A healer's daughter out of Wendholt, pale as the inside of a shell, green-eyed, hair the colour of winter wheat. The songs make a great deal of the hair. They have the wrong end of it. The world is full of beautiful women and not one of them has ever brought a god to ruin by being looked at. What undid Caedmon was that Maelis looked back. Level, unhurried, unafraid, the way she looked at everyone, because she was a healer and she had spent her life reading what a body hides under what it shows. She looked at a god the way she would look at a man swearing his shoulder did not hurt, and she saw straight through him, and she was not impressed. No one had ever not been impressed by him. That was the wound. Not love. Insult, dressed as love, festering.

She was married, which the god took as a detail. The husband was Gradus.

He was nobody. That has to be said plainly, because the statues lie about it. He was a plain man who walked. He walked to the far field at dawn and the long way to the well and the longer way round to the hunting when the short way would have done. He had a good horse and rarely troubled it. The village thought him slow. Maelis thought him the only honest thing in the valley, and she had married him for it, and she was right. When other men courted her they performed. Gradus just answered her questions with the true answer instead of the flattering one, and she, who could see through anybody, could not find the bottom of him, because there was no false bottom to find. He was exactly as much as he showed. For a woman who saw through everyone, that was the one thing worth keeping.

What the god did, he did from the edges, because gods do not brawl with farmers. He soured the well. He emptied the hunt so the man walked farther and came home with less. He did not even dirty his own hands with it. He found men of Wendholt who would do a god a favour for a god's promises, a creature called Hewel chief among them, who poisoned the water and carried the rumours, and those men are the great-grandfathers in spirit of every quisling who has read a poisoned Litany since. Caedmon meant to make Maelis a widow by slow degrees and then arrive, kindly, with comfort. It is almost funny, how badly it went. Almost.

She found out. Of course she found out, she saw through everyone. And she did the worst possible thing with the knowing, which was to act on it alone, because she was certain, and her certainty was the one flaw in her clear sight. A hard winter had come early, the god's doing, and frozen the ford, and stranded Gradus across the river. So Maelis crossed the ice by herself to reach the temple on the far bank and beg the Litany for protection, that being the one power that sat above a single god's spite. The god who answered her at the altar was Caedmon, wearing a borrowed face. She knew him at once. She refused him to his face and would not kneel, and going back across the ice he had made, the ice gave way, and she went under.

He never touched her. Let that stand, because it matters and because both sides have reasons to muddy it. She died refusing a god, with his fingerprints on the weather and not on her. Gradus, racing the early dark to get home, found her under the ice.

Whether she felt anything for the god in her last refusing, I do not know, and I have read everything. Whether the god felt love or only appetite, the gods themselves do not agree, and they were there. Her sister Wyn set down a testament before she died, generations gone now, and it is the truest thing we have, and even Wyn only says this: that her sister was kinder than she was beautiful, and very beautiful, and should not have gone alone. I copy it out when my hands are tired and it steadies them.

What Gradus did after is the only part nobody argues about, though they get the manner of it wrong, because the truth is stranger than the song. The man walked everywhere. Walked to the well, walked to the field, rode that good horse of his about twice a year and grudged it. And the night he set out to kill a god, he saddled the horse he never rode and rode it full speed into the high country, because for the first time in his life walking was too slow, and if you knew the man you understand that this is the most frightening sentence in the whole history. He rode the horse to death. It died under him within sight of the pass, the loyal foolish thing, spent on a grief that was not its own. Then he went the last of the way on foot, in the snow, which was the one thing he had, and the god who thought a horseless man would surely fall back turned and found him still coming. It was not a duel. He was no swordsman. He had his grandfather's rusted blade off the wall over the door and he used it like an axe on a stump, with no skill and the whole weight of a ruined life behind it, and he did not stop swinging when stopping would have been enough. That is the whole of the miracle, and it is not a miracle, it is only a man who would not stop, which turns out to be the most frightening thing in the world. The blade is still with us, or a hundred liars' copies of it are. We call it by his name now.

## Three — The sentence that backfired

The Crowns sat in judgment, because a god was dead and the law howled for it. They could have wiped Gradus out of the world like a wet thumb on chalk. Seraphine talked them out of it, the mother of the dead boy, who had looked at the whole rotten business and seen whose fault it was first. She argued for a sentence instead. They thought they were being merciful by being cruel. They sentenced him to walk without end, a hundred thousand miles and more, never resting, until they chose to relent.

It did not grind him down. It built him. Every mile put iron in him. The lean frame went to cord and the cord went to something harder, his wind turned bottomless, he stopped aging. He took to carrying weight as he walked, a stone, a pack, a dead man's burden, and the weight only fed him. The punishment was a forge and nobody had meant it to be.

There were reasons the judges did not know about. Valdric, god of earned strength, had never admired a mortal more in his life, and could not square his own creed with condemning the one man who had ever lived it to the letter, so each night he folded a little of his strength into the road dust where Gradus would walk. And under Valdric's gift ran the older thing, the Elder Road itself, which answers walking and always has, and which Gradus was walking as it had not been walked in an age. Seraphine gave too. She guarded his soul against the long dark so the miles would not hollow him out, and she did one thing more, quietly, that mattered more than all of it, which I will come to.

And then, one day, having walked past every league and every map and every place a road has any business ending, Gradus kept walking, off the edge of the world, into the country of the dead. The Sunless Court. Understand what that is, because the temples lie about it to sell you their side of it: it is not a heaven and it is not a hell, it is both, the one place every soul goes when the breath stops, carved into regions under the different gods, the lucky dead in the soft places and the rest in the hard ones, and the High Crown over the whole of it. Everyone you have ever buried is down there somewhere, sorted. That is the part that should keep you up at night.

Here is what no one else will tell you, and it is the whole strangeness of the man. Gradus did not die. The dead arrive in the Court because they are dead; that is the only door in. Gradus walked in alive, on his own two feet, the only living thing that has ever stood in the country of the dead, and that, not any holy bloodline, is what makes him the thing he is. How he did it, not one of the Crowns can say, and they have argued it for centuries. My own guess, for what a scribe's guess is worth, is that no god let him in at all, and he simply walked the Elder Road to its end, by a power that was never theirs to give or refuse. But that is a guess, and I have promised you only the parts that cost me.

Maelis was already there, because Maelis was dead, drowned under the ice, and the dead go where the dead go. But when she went under, Seraphine, who sits with the dying and holds the gentle dead, caught her soul before it could scatter into the common dark and hid it close, because Caelvorn in his grief would have unmade her on sight to hurt the husband worse. So the two of them came to the same country by opposite doors, the man walking in alive and the woman carried in dead, and they could not find one another. We will get there. It is the saddest thing in the book and I am not ready.

## Four — The grieving king and the coin

Word reached the High Crown that his son was dead by a mortal hand, and Caelvorn did not weep the way his wife wept. He went cold, and the cold has not lifted since, in him or in the world.

It is easy to call him the villain and be done. Half the resistance does. I will not let you have it that cheap, because the cheap version is a lie and lies are what I am trying to scrape off. A man killed his child. By the oldest law there is, no mortal may raise a hand to a god, and one had, and the survivors were not sorry. They were proud. Before long they would put the murderer's face on their money and sing him in their cups. Stand where Caelvorn stands for one moment. A father robbed. A king watching a death-cult bloom around the thief. He is wrong, monstrously, in what his grief does to the innocent. He is not wrong that he was robbed. Hold both of those or you understand nothing.

He swore an oath he named the Anathema, that the whole world would pay for his son's blood and that Gradus would pay most and longest, his name burned out, his memory salted, every step he ever took turned to misery. And he made the oath real the only way a king-god can. He turned his own voice on his people. The Litany that had nursed them for a thousand years stopped nursing and started biting. Some dawns it goes silent. Some dawns it lies. The blessings curdled. And a cold that should not be began to bleed outward from the place his son had died, eating the lovely country a league at a time, freezing the green and the gold of it, working its slow way up the Road toward the rest of us. It has not reached most of the world yet. That is the mercy and the dread of it both. The world is still beautiful, rivers and red deserts and meadows to break your heart, and a man walking it can almost forget that the far end is dying, until he feels the wrong chill at dawn and remembers which way the cold is coming from.

Here is the part the king did not foresee, and it is the heart of everything. The people had to choose. For the first time in a thousand years the dawn voice could not be trusted, and folk who had never decided anything had to decide everything, and most of them were terrified, because freedom is a sickening thing when you have never had it. They had no king of their own. The gods had seen to that long ago. So they reached back and grabbed the one mortal who had ever told a god no and lived in the memory of it, the very name the High Crown had sworn to erase, and they made it the most-said word in the world. They struck their coin and called it Gradus. Every coin a small defiance, a name in every hand and on every tongue, a thing the king cannot silence because it is in everyone's pocket. He wanted the name gone. They made it their money.

The songs do not like to mention who first saw the use in a dead hero. It was the hero's own brother. Aldred, who had thought Gradus a fool while the man breathed and found him precious only once he was a corpse and a story. Aldred struck early coin and kept rather more of it than was holy, and his line grew fat as the keepers of the legend, minor gentry now, the Strane family, who have sold the Strider's memory for generations and named their sons to match it. So even the coin of the resistance, if you turn it to the light, carries the thumbprint of one bitter man's profit. I find that more honest than the songs do. Most holy things have a grift somewhere near the root.

## Five — The two wars nobody up here can see

The thing the whole shivering world does not know is that its dead are not resting.

Down in the Court, in that lightless country, two wars are being fought, and we feel the tremors up here and call them weather. Time runs strange down there, I am told, faster or slower than ours, so that a day of theirs might be a year of ours or a heartbeat, and no Account agrees on which.

Gradus fights the loud one. He is loose in the king's own house, the one thing in it the king cannot govern, kept whole by Valdric's strength and Seraphine's guard on his soul. He breaks what Caelvorn builds in the dark and drives toward the throne, and he searches, always, for his wife, and here is the cruelty of it. He does not know she was hidden on purpose. He thinks he simply has not walked far enough yet. So he walks. And the very thing that makes him unkillable, that he will not stop, is the thing that keeps him from her, because she is hidden in stillness and concealment, and you cannot reach stillness by force. He could find her tomorrow if he stood still and listened. He has never once in his existence stood still. There is the whole tragedy of the man, and a Wakeful scribe could weep over it if a Wakeful scribe had the time.

Maelis fights the quiet one. She would not be kept safe and idle. With the same eye that saw through a god, she reads what Caelvorn did to the Litany from the inside, understands the Anathema better than any soul alive or dead, and reaches up through the broken places of the world into the few who can hear her, feeding them clear sight, building a thing the gods have not noticed. The woman history barely remembers, the god-killer's drowned wife, is the architect of the whole rebellion, and the great joke of it, if it is a joke, is that her famous husband is the loud distraction and she is the war. She could end her loneliness any day by showing herself to the man who walks the dark calling her name. She does not, because the moment she does, the king finds her, and the work dies with her. She has chosen the work. So far.

The two wars split the heavens. Seraphine shelters her son's killer and works against her own husband, and pays for every mercy. Valdric upholds the law that damns Gradus while feeding him strength on the sly, his creed and his heart at odds for the first time in forever, and his own son, Mordric, took that creed of strength and bent it into a leash and gave it to the throne. Myrren serves no one but her own hunger to know how an impossible thing ends, and would watch the world burn for the answer. And Aelric, who saw all of this coming and was laughed off, has gone quietly done with his own kind, and wants mortals free of the gods entire.

The door Caedmon cracked, walking down in flesh, never shut all the way, and things come through it now. A ragged man walks the Trodden Lands who knows your name before you give it and finds the apocalypse mildly amusing. The Vagabond, they call him. He is a son of Aelric, and his help is real and his loyalty is to his father's war and not to you, and a war that size has always been willing to spend a small life. Through the same door comes his shadow, the hunter Mordric, the Iron Hound, Valdric's son, who runs down the ones the dead reach into. He does not always kill them. Sometimes he breaks them and turns them and keeps them, which is worse.

## Six — The world you actually have to walk

What the living have is a long Road and the five great Reaches it crosses, from the lake-town of Oppidum at its head down to the place at its end where a god bled and the ground went wrong. And let me say a thing the grim songs never bother with: it is beautiful out there. A clear lake under green hills. A forest loud with rivers. A desert that turns to red gold at evening and breaks your heart with it. The Road runs through real loveliness for most of its length, and that is the cruelty of the cold coming up from the far end, because you are not watching a ruin die. You are watching a paradise. To walk the Road is the whole of the faith and the whole of the fight, and the wrongness deepens the nearer you come to the wound, so the country itself tells you how close you are by how little of its beauty is left.

The lands breed their own kinds of folk, and a man who has only known one Reach is forever surprised by the next. The lake people are slow and close-handed and sing through their noses. The forest people go barefoot half the year and trust no one who cannot name a tree. The desert people wrap their faces and bury their dead standing up and will feed a stranger before they will tell him their own name. They squabble and look down on one another the way neighbours do everywhere, and the songs pretend they are all one brave people of the Road, which is a lie, but a useful one. They are not one people. They are a great many, and the only thing they truly share is a god they did not ask for and a coin with a dead man's face on it.

There are no kings. There never were. The Litany was the only ruler the world ever had, the gods speaking direct through the dawn, every town simply obeying, and that is exactly why the poisoning of it gutted everything. The people did not lose a king. They lost the only master they had ever known, all at once, and most of them have no idea how to rule themselves and are making a poor frightened job of learning. Towns that once had a temple-appointed Warden now scrape by under a council, or a Choir, or whoever the Footsworn trust this month.

The dead reach into the living, and the ones they reach are called vessels. There is no single chosen one, whatever the hopeful songs want. Over the long centuries the dead have touched many. Most never wake to it, or break under it. Some woke and were hunted down. Some were turned, and serve the throne with the gift still burning in them, which is the worst weapon the king owns. And here is the ugly secret of the turning, the one I had to learn from a man who nearly went that way himself: it is not strength that fails them. It is hope. The Hound does not break a vessel's body. He waits out their hope and finishes off whatever is left. Which is why the towns the cold has touched, full of folk drinking against the fear, in hock to the Bank to their grandchildren, chewing numbweed to feel a little less of everything, are really just full of dry kindling. Despair is the tinder the corruption wants. Mind that, in yourself, more than you mind any monster. Two kinds of waking are known. Some feel it in the body, an endurance that work cannot drain, which is the man's gift. Some feel it in the words, a clear sight the lie cannot fool, which is the woman's. Temperaments, more than anything, and they do not split as neatly as the singers pretend.

The Footsworn rally to the name on the coin. The Hollow Choir reads the poisoned dawn. And between them, never speaking their name aloud, move the few who carry Maelis's clear sight and suspect the hopeful, terrible truth, that the dead are still in the fight. The Wakeful, we are called. I am one, in case the bitterness had not given me away.

The hardest lesson of the Road waits in the dead temple-city at its middle, the Ruins, where the corrupted vessels walk, the Hollowed, each one a person who could have been a hero and got emptied out instead. And there, holding the dead city, is the worst of them. Before this age the Footsworn had a hope, a vessel who walked farther down the Road than anyone since Gradus, so far they sang him the Second Strider and swore he would reach the Mile and bring our dead ones home. He went into the city seeking what it knew, a way down, a way to break the Anathema, and he forced it, the way his patron would have, and the Iron Hound broke him there and turned him. He is the first of the Hollowed now, and he is not raving. He is gentle. He will offer you rest, and tell you that every vessel who fights only makes the world's pain worse, that it was Gradus's defiance that froze everything, that the kind thing is to lie down and let the gods have their order back. The horror of him is that he is not lying about the pain. He is only wrong about the cure. I have spoken to a man who nearly took the rest he offered, and what frightened that man was not the monster's hands. It was the monster's kindness.

At the very end of the Road lies the Bleeding Mile, where the god died and the ground drank him and went wrong, where the beasts grew swollen and frenzied on divine blood and the air still carries a rage that never cooled. Same place where Gradus, his killing done, kept walking off the edge of the world. A god died there and a man vanished there and something of the god lingers there yet, never wholly dead, wanted by the father who lost him and the veiled goddess who would cut him open to learn and the heretics who would kneel to him. No one walks the Mile unready and walks back out. It is the threshold of the dark country below, and the door, when it opens, opens there.

And there is the matter of what calling a vessel takes, when they have walked far enough to be noticed. Four of them bind to a Crown as an ally, the swordsman to Valdric, the spell-worker to Myrren, the bowman to Aelric, the mercy-worker to Seraphine, and each god helps for reasons of their own and none of those reasons is quite your reasons. One binds by an oath so total it is more a marriage, the paladin, and never forget the only god who ever raised holy warriors was the High Crown, so the armored fists of the poisoned dawn are paladins too. And one binds to no god at all, the Unsworn, who carry knives and answer to no morning and draw what they draw from the old hum in the road that is older than heaven. To take a calling is not to kneel. It is to pick whose quarrel you have joined, or, if you are Unsworn, to say you will join none of theirs.

## Coda

So much for the long memory. It ends here, and a cold ordinary morning begins.

It begins small, the way these things do. In the lake-town of Oppidum, at the head of the Road, a young man with a famous and slightly disgraceful surname has come to walk the holy way and be worthy of a name his own family cheapened, and he is six days in and bitterly unimpressed with the ale. His name is Cassian. He does not yet know what he is. He will, by tomorrow's dawn, and so will the High Crown, and after that the boy's life stops being his own.

The dawn is about to lie, and Cassian Strane, gods help him, is the only one in the square who is going to say so out loud.
