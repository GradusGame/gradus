# Founding Chapter — The Killing of the God

*(A full dramatized chapter of the deep past, tight third on Gradus, centuries before Cassian. Written to VOICE-AND-CRAFT.md. This is the chronicle's seven sentences made into the thing that actually happened. Canon: Gradus rides, against his whole nature, and kills with a plain sword that the world will later call by his name.)*

They buried Maelis on the second day, when the ground by the chapel had thawed enough to take her, and Gradus stood through it and said the words and did not weep, which the village took for strength and was in fact a man who had not yet understood that she was not coming back.

He understood it that night. He sat by the cold hearth because he had not thought to light it, and somewhere between the dark and the dawn the size of the thing arrived in him all at once, the way the sea arrives in a holed boat, and he made a sound he had never made before and hoped never to make again. Then he was quiet. And in the quiet he did the arithmetic that grief does, the cruel clean sums, and they came out the same every way he added them. The early winter that should not have come. The well gone bad. The hunt gone empty. The neighbours crossing the road. A god's face at the altar where his wife had gone to beg for help, and his wife under the ice an hour later. He had been a slow man all his life. He was not slow about this. By the time the window greyed he knew exactly whose hand had been in all of it, and he knew the hand belonged to a god, and he found that he did not care that it belonged to a god.

There was a sword over the door. There had always been a sword over the door, his grandfather's, a plain thing, more rust than edge, hung up the way old men hang up their wars. Gradus took it down. It was clumsy in his hand. He had never once swung it. He did not swing it now, only carried it out into the yard where the horse stood.

The horse. That was the part the village would never understand, in all the songs they made after, because they did not know the man. Gradus walked. Gradus walked everywhere, to the far field and the well and the long way to the hunting when the short way would serve, and the good horse stood in the field most days inventing insults about him, because he loved to walk and a man who loves to walk does not ride. Maelis used to tease him for it.

He saddled the horse. He had to think about how. And then the man who walked got up onto the back of the animal he never rode, and turned its head toward the high country where a god would run to reach the thin place between the worlds, and he put his heels in, and he rode.

He rode the way only a man with nothing left rides. Not well. He was no horseman any more than he was a swordsman. But he rode without mercy, the mercy he had spent on that animal every day of its life now gone the way the rest of his gentleness had gone, into the cold sum by the hearth. He rode it through the day and into the dark and into the next grey day, and the horse screamed at him sometimes the way horses do when they are being asked for the thing past the last thing, and he asked anyway. Up. On. Faster. The hooves threw the early snow back in long wings. The breath of the beast came ragged and then bloody. And ahead of him, far ahead and then less far, a figure in a borrowed shape ran for the high pass, glancing back, and its face when it glanced back was beginning, for the first time in its endless life, to learn what fear was.

Caedmon had never been chased. Caedmon had never needed to learn that a creature can want a thing more than it wants to live. He saw the horse coming and laughed, because no horse could keep that pace and live, and he was right, the horse could not. Within sight of the pass it died. Its great heart simply stopped and it went down mid-stride and threw Gradus into the snow and lay there steaming, dead, the most loyal foolish thing in the valley spent at last on a man's rage.

Gradus got up. He had lost the sword in the fall and he found it and got up, and he was a long way still from the god, on foot now, in deep snow, a slow plain man with a rusty blade and no breath left. And Caedmon, watching from up the slope, felt the fear go out of him, because surely now. Surely a horseless man in the snow would fall back. Surely this was the end of the absurd thing.

The man kept coming.

He came at a walk, because the walk was the one thing he had, the one thing that was truly his, and he had more of it in him than any god could account for. He came up the white slope one step and then the next and then the next, and he did not hurry and he did not stop, and the not-stopping was worse to watch than any charge would have been. Caedmon shouted at him. Threatened, then promised, then, at the last, when the man was close enough to see his eyes, begged, because a god who has worn flesh can learn to beg the same as anyone. None of it touched the plain cold face coming up the hill.

It was not a duel. The songs make it a duel and the songs are liars. It was a graceless, ugly, human thing. Gradus reached him and the god struck at him with power enough to break a wall and Gradus took it and kept his feet, because the road had been pouring its strength into him for two hard days without either of them knowing, and he swung the rusty blade the way you swing an axe at a stump, with no skill at all, only the whole weight of a ruined life behind it, and again, and again, past the point of need, past the point of sense, until the snow was not white anymore and the borrowed face had stopped wearing anyone, and a thing that was never supposed to be able to die lay dead in a drift with a farmer's grandfather's sword buried in it.

Gradus knelt down in the red snow. He was not triumphant. That is the part the statues get most wrong, the ones that show him with his foot on the god and his chin up. He knelt down because his legs had finally finished, and he looked at the dead god, and the dead god did not bring Maelis back, and nothing was ever going to bring Maelis back, and he had known that the whole time and ridden a good horse to death and killed a god anyway, for nothing, because grief does not do arithmetic that comes out to nothing, it only does the sums it can.

He pulled the sword free. He did not know yet that the gods were watching, or that they had already begun to argue about what to do with him, or that the blade in his hand would be a holy thing in a thousand hands he would never meet, prayed over, fought over, faked by every blacksmith with a debt, called by his own name down all the years to come. He only knew it was cold, and that he was a long way from home, and that there was no longer any particular reason to go back to a home with no one in it.

So he did the only thing he had ever been good at.

He started walking.
